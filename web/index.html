      How to practise

      • Go slowly. Calligraphy is drawn more than written; a long stroke takes about a second.
      • With a pointed pen, press only on the way down and release before the turn. With a broad nib, never turn the pen: hold it at the hand's angle and let the direction make the thick and thin.
      • Keep the downstrokes parallel to each other (and to the slant lines, where the hand leans); check them after each letter.
      • Lift the pen where the model does (the numbers show the strokes); a smooth join is better than a forced one.
      • Start with the dotted keys on the Practice tab: each hand marks the letters to learn first. Then the round letters, then the long ones, then capitals.
      • Pointed-pen hands (Copperplate, Spencerian, Modern) take their thick and thin from pressure; broad-nib hands from the direction, with the nib held at one angle all the time.
      • For words, copy the minim words first (minimum, nun), then a pangram, then your own texts; download a copybook and practise on paper too.

      The layers

      LayerWhat it does
      renderYour strokes and the model's become the same 96 × 96 maps: ink, the pen's path, pen width, writing time, direction (down, up, left, right). Same code for both: the Siamese part.
      E1, E21 × 1 convolutions with shared weights: solid ink, shaded ink (pen wider than 2.25 px), the model's hairlines.
      B1, B2Max over 3 × 3: the tolerance band, 2 px for tracing and 4 px for copying; direction and shade only 1 px.
      C1, C2Comparison maps, each an AND or AND-NOT written as clip(a + b − 1): on or off the path, covered or missed, same or opposite direction, too early or too late, thick where thin.
      Z, RPixel counts, then fixed ratios: stay, cover, direction, order slip, shade hit, hairline slip.
      Z2, R2, SCopy game: moments of the thin-pen path give its slant (slope of x on y) and size (variance of y), against the model's.
      Q, F, OThe marks (shape, ductus, shading, form), the faults in points (the tip names the largest), and the overall score.

      How it's made

      A calligraphy teacher whose network was written by hand: 191 edit lines, each with a reason, and nothing trained.

      How a letter is marked

      1. The model letter is stored as pen strokes in writing order, at the copperplate slant of 55°. Its thick and thin parts follow the pointed-pen rule: pressure only while the pen moves down along the slant.
      2. Your strokes and the model's go through the same steps: drawn on a 96 × 96 grid as maps of ink, the pen's path, pen width, writing time and direction.
      3. Hand-written layers widen the maps into a tolerance band and compare them pixel by pixel: your ink inside or outside the band, the model covered or missed, the same or the opposite direction, written too early or too late, thick where the model is thick.
      4. The counts become measures, and fixed weights turn them into the marks. In the copy game your letter is first fitted onto the model (place, size and slant), so shape and ductus mark the strokes themselves; slant and size, measured before the fit, make the form mark.
      5. Text is cut into letters at the boundaries between them (a joined stroke is split where it crosses one) and each letter is marked on its own; the report adds them up.

      Is this a real model?

      In structure, yes: convolutions, pooling and linear layers, run by an engine that starts every weight at zero and fills it from a plain-text edit list. The same encoder reads your letter and the model's.

      Nothing was learned. Every weight is a rule written by hand, such as "your ink here and no model ink near it means off the path". It is a scoring method written in the form of a neural network: every score can be explained, and a perfect copy always scores 100. It has not seen real handwriting; the model letters are one hand's version of copperplate.

      How the page is designed

      • Play first. Practice opens on a letter ready to trace. Explanations live in their own tabs.
      • A notebook, not a form. Text is laid out on ruled pages with a margin line, the way you would practise on paper; long texts turn into pages, each checked when you finish it.
      • Precision, not position. In the copy game you can write anywhere on the page; the network fits your letter onto the model before it marks the strokes.
      • The eye travels sideways. When copying text, the model page sits beside yours, row level with row.
      • Feedback at two sizes. One tip for a letter; for a text, the report adds up hundreds of letters into a few things to work on and the letters to practise next.
      • Take it home. Every text and every letter can become a printable tracing sheet or copybook.
      • Ten hands, one network. The network compares any two sets of strokes, so a new hand needs only its letters, its guidelines and its pen; broad nibs are drawn as the shape their edge sweeps.
      • Measured against the sheets. Each hand's letters were authored from its exemplar sheet in the sheet's stroke order: the sheet measured first (x-height in nib widths, slant, nib angle, widths), then every row set beside the letters at the same size. Never traced, and never taken from a font: a font has no stroke order.
      • Built for a pen. The first stylus stroke ignores the palm, the tools stay in reach at the bottom, Focus gives the notebook the whole screen, and Lock holds the page still under your hand.
